# FSGUI

Copyright © 2025 Frode Solheim

fsgui is FS-UAE's Python UI toolkit, a client of fsapp's surface API, version 1 (`fsapp.surfaceapi`, described in [Surface API.md](../../Docs/Development/Surface%20API.md)): its windows are surfaces, and it uses the surface API's events, drawing, timers and UI frames. It sits above fsapp and below fsemu and the Amiga-specific top, and uses nothing of fsapp but the surface API: its modules import each other relatively and, from outside fsgui, only the standard library and `fsapp.surfaceapi` (checked by `od-fs/tests/fsgui/test_imports.py`). So fsgui can be vendored: a copy inside another package (`plugin/_vendor/fsgui`) works alongside FS-UAE's own, with its own windows and scale. fsgui has only a Python side: native code that the toolkit needs goes into the surface API.

fsgui gets its own API object (`fsgui/internal/api.py`), and `fsgui_init()` sets fsgui's frame as its UI frame callback, which lays out, paints and commits the dirty windows. Each window draws through a canvas of its surface (`DrawingContext`); 9-patch images are fsgui's own (`fsgui/image.py` reads their markers from the image's pixels and draws the nine parts), and `Image.tint` returns a tinted copy.

The host window's chrome (the main window, its title bar and borders, and the file dialog) is not fsgui's: it belongs to the top (`app/`), built with fsgui's windows and widgets. The top tells fsgui where the client area is, inside the chrome (`fsgui.windowmanager.set_client_origin` and `set_client_size`, in design units).

The program tells fsgui where its fonts and images are, with `fsgui.set_resource_dirs(fonts=..., images=...)` before the first window: FS-UAE gives it the `Fonts` and `Images` directories of its resources.

The modules and their order (fsapp → fsgui → fsemu → the top) are described in [Modules.md](../../Docs/Development/Modules.md), with the check that enforces the order.

## Windows and input

Each window is one of fsapp's surfaces. fsapp stacks the surfaces, hit tests the pointer between them, raises and focuses on a press and decides which window gets the keys, the same for every copy of fsgui; a window says what it is and asks:

- **Roles and bands:** given when a window is created, and fixed: a window is a top-level unless `Window(..., role=Role.CHILD, parent=...)` or `role=Role.POPUP` makes it a child or popup of another window, or `role=Role.OVERLAY` an overlay: above every band, taking no pointer input and no focus (notifications). A top-level's band (`band=`: `Band.BACKGROUND`, `Band.NORMAL` by default, or `Band.PANEL`) is the part of the stacking order it stays in when raised; the main window's title bar and borders are panel windows that are not focusable. `Role` and `Band` are in `fsapp.surfaceapi.v1`. A window and its children and popups form a surface tree, which stacks and takes keyboard focus together: children above their parent, popups above the rest of the tree. A hidden window hides its children and popups, and hiding or destroying a window closes its popups. A window's position is in the client area whatever its role, and a parent's move does not move its children or popups; a window that has child surfaces places them in `place_surfaces`. A `SurfaceWindow(title, adopt)` has no buffer and shows a surface created elsewhere (the emulation surface) as its child and key target: `adopt` is given the window's surface and returns the content surface, a child of it. `get_surface()` gives a window's surface (`fsapp.surfaceapi.v1.Surface`) for calls that take one, such as the top's file dialog.
- **Raising and focus:** `show()` makes a window visible, asks fsapp to raise it (`raise_()`) and, if it is focusable (`set_focusable`), asks for keyboard focus for its tree (`focus()`). A press raises and focuses too. A window draws its title bar as focused from `FocusIn` and `FocusOut`, which are given to the top-level window of the tree.
- **Pointer events:** each window gets its own pointer events and converts their positions to design units relative to the window; the window manager finds the widget under the pointer and calls its hooks: `on_mouse_enter` and `on_mouse_leave` (also when the pointer leaves the window), `on_left_press`, `on_left_release` and `on_left_click`, `on_right_press` and `on_right_release`, and `on_wheel(dx, dy)`; `get_click_count()` gives the press's click count (2 for a double click). A pressed widget gets the motion and the release wherever the pointer goes, since fsapp keeps sending them to the window the press started in, so title bar drags and sliders work. A widget can hand its press to a widget in another window (`WindowManager.transfer_mouse_tracking`), as a choice hands it to its menu. `WindowManager.get_mouse_position()` and `mouse_moved` give the pointer in the client area, wherever it is.
- **Keys:** the window that has the keys gets `on_key_down`, `on_key_up` and `on_navigation` (from gamepads and the navigation keys). There is no widget focus and no text input yet.
- **Popups:** a popup (`Role.POPUP`) may reach outside its parent, gets the keys while it is visible, and gets `on_outside_click()` when a press lands outside every open popup. No other window gets that press, so a click on the title bar or the emulated display closes a menu without pressing what is under it. The popup closes itself: a choice's menu closes on it, and on Escape.

## Design units and pixels

fsgui's API is in design units, as designed at a display scale of 1: the positions and sizes of windows and widgets, minimum sizes, padding, gaps, borders, font sizes, mouse positions, and the coordinates a widget paints with. They are floats (integers are accepted), and nothing outside fsgui converts them for the display scale. A getter returns exactly what was set, or what the layout assigned, at any scale: `set_width(201)` then `get_width()` gives 201. Mouse positions are the pixel position divided by the scale, so they compare directly with widgets' geometry.

The layout computes minimum sizes and shares space in design units, without rounding. Text and images are measured in pixels and become design units rounded up to a whole unit, so that they fit after rounding. When the scale changes, windows and widgets keep their design geometry: the windows take surfaces of the new size, are laid out again (sizes measured from text may change) and are painted.

fsgui creates every surface with the scale support "any" (`fsapp.surfaceapi.v1.ScaleSupport.ANY`): it scales what it draws itself, so fsapp gives its surfaces the display scale as their scale and draws them pixel for pixel, and their positions, sizes and pointer positions are pixels of the host window. fsgui has one scale (`fsgui/scale.py`): the display scale, rounded to 1/120, taken from the API object's `display_scale` at start and then from its surfaces' configures (a configure with another scale rescales the windows). Pixels come only when drawing, by the rules in `scale.py`:

- **Edge rounding:** a rectangle becomes pixels by rounding each of its edges (the edge times the scale, relative to its window's origin, halves away from zero), and its size in pixels is the difference between its rounded edges. Adjacent widgets share their edges, so they never leave a gap or overlap, but equal sizes can differ by a pixel where the scale times the value is not whole. A window's surface size is rounded as the far edges of a rectangle at 0, so that what is drawn at its edges is inside it, and its position is rounded on its own, so a window keeps its size in pixels when it moves.
- **Lines** (outlines, borders, separators, line widths) are their design width times the scale, rounded down, and at least 1 pixel, drawn inside the rounded edge of their rectangle, the same width wherever they are: a 1-unit line is 1, 1, 1 and 2 pixels at 125, 150, 175 and 200 %.
- **9-patch images** draw their corners and edges at a whole factor, the width of a 1-unit line, without smoothing, and stretch only their centre. For an even factor, `<name>@2x.9p.png` beside the image is drawn at half the factor when it exists.
- **Fonts** are loaded at their size times the scale, rounded to whole pixels. An image made for a scale of 1 is drawn into its rounded rectangle (`draw_image_scaled`); `draw_image` draws an image at its own size in pixels, such as an icon rendered from SVG at the scale.

A widget paints with a `DrawingContext` (`Widget.create_dc()`), whose calls take design units relative to the widget and convert them by these rules, so that what a widget draws matches its rounded rectangle. `dc.draw_text_centred` centres text in a rectangle in pixels, and wrapped text is wrapped at the pixels its width has wherever it is, as `Label` measures it. To draw with pixel values (a level meter, a pixel-exact preview), use its pixel view, `dc.pixel_view`: the same calls in pixels, relative to the widget's rectangle in the surface's pixels. `dc.scale` is the scale, `dc.rect_to_view_pixels(position, size)` gives a rectangle in design units in the pixel view's pixels, and `dc.design_line_to_pixels(width)` a line's width in pixels. Values in pixels are typed `PixelPosition`, `PixelSize` and `PixelRect` (`fsgui/types.py`).

Design values that are multiples of 4 land on whole pixels at 125, 150, 175 and 200 % (5, 6, 7 and 8 pixels for 4 units), so rows of widgets laid out on a 4-unit grid stay even at those scales. The grid is recommended, not enforced.
